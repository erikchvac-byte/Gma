# Discovery-Call Script — WA Dispensary Price-Intelligence Validation

Purpose: find out whether WA dispensary owners/managers feel enough pain about competitor pricing
that they have already spent time or money trying to solve it — the #1 unknown gating the B2B
direction. This is a learning call, not a sales call.

## The one rule

Do not describe Gma's or your idea until the very end (and only if you've earned it). The goal is to
hear about their real behavior, not their opinion of your product. Opinions and "that sounds useful"
are worthless; past actions and spent money are gold.

## Who to talk to

- Owner, GM, or whoever actually sets prices/promotions. Not a budtender.
- Start with stores in your existing coverage cluster (North Puget Sound) so you could actually serve
  them if this works.
- Aim for 5–10 conversations. Patterns matter more than any single call.

## Opening (30 seconds, low pressure)

"Hi — I'm Erik, I run gmaslist.com, a local WA cannabis deal site. I'm not selling anything today. I'm
trying to understand how stores like yours handle pricing against competitors, because I might build
something in that area and I don't want to build the wrong thing. Could I ask you a few questions about
how you do it now? Ten minutes."

Then stop pitching. Everything after this is questions.

## Core questions — past behavior and effort (the meat)

Ask these, then shut up and let them talk. Follow the energy; dig where they get animated or annoyed.

1. Walk me through how you set prices on your top-selling categories — flower, say. How do you decide?
2. When you set that price, do you look at what nearby stores are charging? Tell me about the last time
   you did that.
   - How did you actually check? (Listen for: menus, driving by, budtender gossip, a spreadsheet, POS
     reports, nothing.)
   - How long did that take? How often do you do it?
   - Who does it — you, or someone on staff?
3. Tell me about a time a competitor's pricing or a promo caught you off guard. What happened? What did
   it cost you?
4. How do you keep track of it over time, if at all? Can you see whether a competitor's been creeping
   their prices down over a month?
5. Have you ever paid for anything to help with this — Headset, a consultant, a tool, an employee's
   time? What did you use, and did you stick with it or drop it? Why?
6. If you could wave a wand and know one thing about your competitors that you can't easily see today,
   what would it be?

## What you're listening for

Real signal (pain is expensive and unsolved):
- They already spend real time or money on it (staff hours, a paid tool, a VA).
- They tell a specific, recent story with a cost attached ("we sat 20% high on carts for two weeks").
- They've tried a solution and it fell short — they're actively unhappy with the status quo.
- They get animated, interrupt you, go on a rant.

False signal (be skeptical):
- "Yeah that sounds useful." (Opinion about the future = worthless.)
- "We just kind of eyeball it, it's fine." (No pain = no sale.)
- Polite, low-energy, generic answers with no specific story.

If nobody has spent time or money on this, that is a finding: the pain may not be strong enough to
pay for. Better to learn it now than after you build.

## Commitment test — only if you heard real signal

Do NOT do this if the call was lukewarm. If they showed real pain, test for a real commitment (this is
what separates a paid pilot / LOI from a worthless "verbal yes"):

Soft-launch the concept in one sentence:
"Here's why I ask — I already collect current, verified shelf prices and deal depth across WA stores.
I'm thinking about turning it into a simple weekly view of exactly where you sit versus your local
competitors. Does that sound like it would've helped with the situations you just described?"

Then push for a real next step, in order of strength:
- Best: "I could set up a paid trial for your store — a small monthly fee for a few weeks so you can
  see if it earns its keep. Would you put, say, $[X] toward trying it?" (Real money = real validation.)
- Good: "If I build this, would you sign a short letter saying you intend to buy at around $[X]/month?
  It's not binding — it just tells me you're serious enough to put your name on it."
- Weak (note but don't over-value): "Can I come back to you in a few weeks with a working version?"

Whatever they say, ask: "Who else do you know who'd have strong feelings about this — could you
introduce me?" A real intro is itself a signal; a vague "sure" is not.

## Hard don'ts

- Don't ask "Would you pay for this?" in the abstract — it invites a polite yes that means nothing.
- Don't explain features. Every minute you talk is a minute you're not learning.
- Don't argue or correct them. Their reality is the data, even when it's inconvenient.
- Don't count compliments as validation. Only money, signatures, and specific past actions count.

## After each call — quick scoring (60 seconds, write it down)

- Do they currently spend time/money on competitor pricing? (yes / no — this is the key line)
- Specific painful story with a cost? (yes / no)
- Real commitment offered? (paid pilot / LOI / intro / nothing)
- One surprising thing they said:
- Would I actually be able to serve this store today (coverage)? (yes / no)

After ~5–10 calls, look at the pattern. If most stores already spend real effort here and several
offered a pilot or LOI, demand is real — proceed. If they mostly shrug, that's your answer too, and
you saved yourself the build.
